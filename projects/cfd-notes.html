The verification habits behind the projects in this portfolio — what I check before believing a force coefficient, and the questions I try to answer before publishing a result. Written down so the reasoning is inspectable, not just the pictures.

01 · The checklist

Six questions every study has to answer

This is the template I use for each study, and the standard the project pages here are written against. A result that cannot answer these is a picture, not a finding.

Table 1Study template — what must be recorded, and why.
#QuestionWhat gets recorded
1QuestionThe physical or design question being investigated — stated before the model is built.
2ModelGeometry, simplifications, governing equations, turbulence and physical models, and what was deliberately left out.
3Numerical setupDomain extents, boundary conditions, mesh strategy, near-wall resolution, solver settings, convergence criteria.
4ResultsFigures with units, labelled axes, stated reference area and reference velocity, and the configuration they belong to.
5VerificationMesh and domain sensitivity, conservation checks, panel/element-count independence, comparison against theory or experiment where it exists.
6LimitationsWhat the model does not establish — written next to the result, not in a footnote elsewhere.
02 · Near-wall treatment

y⁺ is a decision, not a number you report afterwards

The choice between resolving the boundary layer and modelling it with wall functions determines the mesh before anything else. It has to be made from the physics of the question — separation and wall shear stress need resolution; a bulk pressure-drop estimate may not.

Table 2Near-wall targets actually used in this portfolio.
StudyTargetWhy
Formula Student — 2D wing (Fluent)y⁺ < 3Structured mesh with inflation layers on every element; needed for slot-jet and separation behaviour.
Formula Student — 3D wing (SimScale, k–ω SST)y⁺ < 1Resolve wall shear stress rather than model it; verified with a wall y⁺ map, not assumed.
Rocketry — compressible external flowResolution set per caseDensity-based solver with energy equation; drag monitored as the integral quantity of interest.
A stated target is not evidence. The 3D wing study includes a wall y⁺ map showing the achieved distribution across the surfaces — that is the check that matters.

The practical sequence: estimate the flat-plate skin-friction coefficient at the relevant Reynolds number, get the wall shear stress and friction velocity, then size the first cell height for the target y⁺ — and verify the achieved map afterwards, because the estimate is only as good as the velocity scale you assumed.

03 · Convergence

Residuals are necessary, never sufficient

A residual plot falling three orders of magnitude tells you the linear algebra is behaving. It does not tell you the quantity you care about has stopped changing. The projects here therefore monitor the integral output alongside residuals:

  • Formula Student: lift and drag coefficient histories, checked for a flat plateau after the initial transient — the underbody study explicitly shows force-coefficient convergence rather than only residuals.
  • Rocketry: residual target of 1×10⁻⁵ for continuity and energy, plus a stable drag-coefficient plateau.
  • XFOIL screening: the equivalent check is panel-count independence — every candidate was re-run at N = 160 → 300 to confirm the polars were not a discretisation artefact.
The distinction worth keeping

Iterative convergence — has this run settled? Discretisation convergence — would a finer mesh give the same answer? They are different questions, and only the second one says anything about accuracy. A perfectly converged run on a bad mesh is still wrong.

04 · Domain & boundaries

Keep the boundaries away from the physics

Domain sizing is a sensitivity study, not a habit. The 3D wing study sized the domain in chord lengths and stated the reasoning for each face:

Inlet · 3 c

Enough upstream length for the incoming flow to develop and for the body's upstream influence not to reach the boundary.

Outlet · 8 c

The wake needs the most room. Placing the outlet too close forces the wake to exit before it has developed, contaminating the force prediction.

Lateral · 3 c

Limits blockage. Too small a cross-section accelerates the flow around the body and inflates both lift and drag.

Symmetry is the other big lever. The rocketry studies simulate a quarter domain, which is legitimate only while the flow itself is symmetric — a valid assumption at zero incidence, and one that breaks as soon as asymmetric vortex shedding or angle of attack matters. Knowing when a symmetry plane stops being free accuracy is part of using it responsibly.

05 · Turbulence modelling

Why k–ω SST keeps being the right answer here

Every external-aerodynamics study in this portfolio uses k–ω SST, and the reason is specific rather than habitual: these flows are dominated by adverse pressure gradients and separation — multi-element wing flaps near stall, diffuser pressure recovery, fin and body wakes.

SST blends k–ω near the wall, where it resolves the boundary layer without damping functions, into k–ε in the free stream, where standard k–ω is notoriously sensitive to inlet turbulence values. Its shear-stress limiter improves separation prediction under adverse gradients, which is precisely the behaviour being designed against.

What RANS still cannot do

A steady RANS solution gives a time-averaged picture. It does not resolve vortex shedding, buffeting or unsteady load fluctuations, and it tends to be optimistic about reattachment. Where those matter — post-stall wing behaviour, diffuser stall — the honest statement is that the model brackets the trend rather than predicts the instantaneous flow. That is one reason the Formula Student work treats 2D and 3D results as screening and asks lap time, not a single force number, to judge the design.

06 · Interpretation

Reporting coefficients so they can be reused

A force coefficient is meaningless without its reference quantities. Every set here states:

  • Reference area — and whether it is frontal area or planform area. The lap-time simulation explicitly assumes the frontal area entered matches the CFD reference area, because a mismatch silently rescales every result.
  • Reference velocity — the 16 m/s cornering condition is not the 33.3 m/s maximum-speed condition.
  • Sign convention — downforce taken as positive CL throughout the Formula Student work, stated explicitly because the opposite convention is equally common.
  • 2D or 3D — section coefficients per unit span are not comparable to finite-wing coefficients. The 2D sweep reaches CL ≈ 5.4 while the 3D wing reaches ≈ 3.5–4.1; that gap is finite-span physics, not an inconsistency.
  • Configuration identity — which geometry, which incidence, which mesh. The two rocketry studies are kept in separate folders for exactly this reason.
The rule I try to hold

Publish the assumption next to the number. A reader who disagrees with the assumption can then still use the result — and a reviewer can find the mistake. That is worth more than a cleaner-looking plot.
