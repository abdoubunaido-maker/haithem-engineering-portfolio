Riyah.

A wind-turbine design and performance-prediction application. Riyah takes blade-element momentum theory — fast enough to sit inside an optimisation loop — and wraps it in the parts that usually stop people using it: airfoil-polar acquisition, post-stall extrapolation, loss corrections, blade geometry generation and result visualisation. Validated against MEXICO and NREL UAE Phase VI experimental data, and deployed on Streamlit Community Cloud.

01 · Overview

Why build this at all?

Rotor aerodynamics has an awkward gap in it. CFD resolves the flow but is far too expensive to run thousands of times inside a design search. Blade-element momentum theory is cheap enough for that — a full CP–λ curve in a fraction of a second — but using it properly means assembling a lot of unglamorous machinery first: polars at the right Reynolds numbers, extrapolated to angles the wind tunnel never reached, plus tip, hub and high-induction corrections.

Riyah closes that gap. It puts the whole chain behind one interface, so a rotor can be specified, analysed, reshaped and optimised without leaving the tool. It was developed as a final-year project and is built to be used by someone who wants an answer, not by someone who wants to write a BEM solver.

02 · Architecture

How the pieces fit together

The design of the application follows the dependency chain of the physics. Polars are resolved and made complete before the solver runs, because a BEM iteration that walks off the end of a polar table is the most common source of silent nonsense in rotor codes. The optimiser then sits outside the solver and treats it as a black box.

03 · Method

Blade-element momentum theory, properly

BEM equates two independent expressions for the load on an annular ring of the rotor: the momentum the ring extracts from the flow, and the aerodynamic force the blade sections in that ring actually generate. Solving them together gives the induction factors, and from those everything else follows.

Geometry of a blade element

At radius r, the section sees a relative velocity combining the axially retarded wind and the tangentially accelerated rotation:

W = √[U∞(1 − a)]² + [Ωr(1 + a′)]² Eq. 1
φ = arctan U∞(1 − a)Ωr(1 + a′) ,   α = φ − (θpitch + θtwist) Eq. 2

The angle of attack α is what indexes the polar — and it is the reason the polars must cover the full range. At start-up or low tip-speed ratio, α at the inboard stations runs far past stall, well outside any measured table.

The two load expressions

Momentum side, for an annulus of width dr with total loss factor F:

dT = 4πr ρ U∞² a(1 − a) F dr Eq. 3
dQ = 4πr³ ρ U∞ Ω a′(1 − a) F dr Eq. 4

Blade-element side, for B blades of chord c:

dT = ½ B ρ W² c (Cl cos φ + Cd sin φ) dr Eq. 5
dQ = ½ B ρ W² c (Cl sin φ − Cd cos φ) r dr Eq. 6

Note that drag adds to thrust in Eq. 5 but subtracts from torque in Eq. 6. This is why neglecting Cd inflates predicted power — and why polar quality matters as much as the solver. Integrating along the span and non-dimensionalising gives the coefficients the tool reports:

CP = QΩ½ρA U∞3 ,   CT = T½ρA U∞2 ,   λ = ΩRU∞ Eq. 7

The corrections that make it trustworthy

Table 1Corrections applied to the ideal annulus model, and why each is needed.
CorrectionPhysical reasonForm
Prandtl tip loss A finite number of blades sheds tip vortices; the annulus is not independent, and loading must fall to zero at the tip. Ftip = (2/π)·arccos[e−B(R−r)/(2r sinφ)]
Hub loss The same argument at the root, where the blade meets the hub. Fhub = (2/π)·arccos[e−B(r−rhub)/(2r sinφ)]
High induction Above a ≈ 0.4 momentum theory predicts a reversed wake and becomes non-physical (turbulent-wake state). Empirical CT–a relation (Glauert / Buhl type)
Post-stall polars Measured or computed polars stop near stall; the solver still needs values at high α. Viterna-type extrapolation to ±180°
F = Ftip·Fhub. Without the loss factors an ideal BEM code over-predicts power near the tip and approaches the Betz limit too closely.

Feel the momentum theory underneath

Exact one-dimensional actuator-disk results: CP = 4a(1−a)² and CT = 4a(1−a). Teaching illustration of the theory — not Riyah output.

Power peaks at a = 1/3, but thrust keeps climbing towards a = 0.5. That tension — between extracting energy and loading the structure — is the real design problem, and it is why maximum CP is rarely the only objective. Past a ≈ 0.4 the simple theory breaks down, which is exactly where the empirical correction in Table 1 takes over.

04 · Airfoil data

The part that decides whether the answer means anything

A BEM code is only as good as its Cl(α, Re) and Cd(α, Re) tables. Riyah supports three routes to them, because no single source is always available:

  1. Local database

    Curated polars for common wind-turbine sections, ready for immediate use and reproducible between runs.

  2. Fetch or upload

    Pull published data, or bring your own measured or computed tables — including section data from a separate CFD or wind-tunnel campaign.

  3. Generate or predict

    Three interchangeable routes: a built-in panel method, XFOIL at the required Reynolds number, or NeuralFoil for a fast learned estimate. This is the same batch-XFOIL instinct used in the Formula Student airfoil campaign, applied to rotor sections.

  4. Extrapolate to ±180°

    Extend the finite table so the solver never has to guess. Viterna-type extrapolation blends the polar at the stall angle into flat-plate behaviour at high incidence, where Cd tends towards a bluff-body value and Cl falls back towards a sinusoidal form.

Why extrapolation is a modelling choice, not a formality

Different extrapolation models give visibly different answers at start-up and at low tip-speed ratio, where much of the blade is stalled. The extrapolated region is not validated data — it is a physically-motivated assumption. Any result that depends heavily on it should be reported with that caveat, which is why the model used and the original polar range belong in the output metadata.

05 · Blade design

From a target λ to a chord and twist distribution

The design module uses the classical Schmitz optimum-rotor result, which gives a starting geometry directly. Choosing a design tip-speed ratio and a design angle of attack fixes the inflow angle at every station, and from it the chord:

φr = 23 arctan(1λr) ,   λr = λ rR Eq. 8
cr = 8πrB Cl (1 − cos φr) ,   θtwist,r = φr − αdesign Eq. 9

This produces the familiar tapered, twisted blade — wide and heavily twisted at the root, narrow and nearly flat at the tip. It is a starting point: it ignores losses and assumes a single design Cl. The loss-corrected solver then evaluates what the geometry actually delivers, and the optimiser refines it.

The 3D blade view exists for a practical reason. Chord and twist tables hide problems that a rendered blade shows immediately — a root chord that will not fit the hub, a twist distribution too aggressive to manufacture, or a non-monotonic taper that came out of an unconstrained search.

06 · Optimisation

Why differential evolution

The rotor design objective is not smooth. Polars are interpolated tables, the high-induction correction switches branch, stall onset introduces kinks, and the loss factors are transcendental. Gradient methods struggle with that landscape and are easily trapped by the starting guess.

Differential evolution is a population-based, derivative-free search: it mutates candidates by scaled differences between population members, crosses them over, and keeps improvements. It handles multimodal, non-smooth objectives and box constraints naturally — which is exactly the shape of this problem — and each objective evaluation is cheap because the solver is BEM rather than CFD.

Table 2Structure of the design search.
ElementTypical formulation
Design variablesChord and twist at spanwise control stations; design tip-speed ratio; pitch setting
ObjectiveMaximise CP at the design λ, or maximise annual energy production over a wind-speed distribution
ConstraintsChord bounds and smoothness, maximum tip speed (noise), manufacturable twist, root fitting
EvaluationLoss-corrected BEM — fast enough for thousands of candidate evaluations
For a Weibull wind distribution f(v), AEP = 8760·∫P(v)f(v)dv — which rewards a broad CP–λ curve rather than a tall narrow peak, and usually gives a different blade from a single-point CP optimisation.
07 · Validation

Checking the tool against experimental data

A design tool that has never reproduced a measured answer is just a random-number generator with a nice interface. Riyah has built-in validation cases against two published experimental campaigns, chosen because they test different things — and the comparison is scored as an RMSE across the CP curve, not a single matched operating point.

Case A

MEXICO rotor

Model rotor EXperiments In COntrolled conditions — a three-bladed instrumented model rotor measured in a large wind tunnel, with pressure and load data across a range of operating conditions. A well-characterised, largely attached-flow case.

0.028CP RMSE

Tests whether the solver, loss corrections and polar pipeline reproduce measured rotor performance in the regime where BEM is expected to work well.

Case B

NREL UAE Phase VI rotor

A two-bladed, stall-regulated rotor with the S809 section, measured in the NASA Ames 80 ft × 120 ft wind tunnel. The harder test: it exercises the stalled regime, where the answer depends on post-stall polar extrapolation and the high-induction correction rather than clean attached-flow physics.

0.038CP RMSE

A BEM code can look excellent in attached flow and still be badly wrong here — which is exactly why this case is included.

Why RMSE rather than a matched peak

Quoting a single reproduced optimum proves very little — a model can be tuned to hit one point. An RMSE over the whole power-coefficient curve against measurement tests the shape of the prediction, and the higher error on Phase VI (0.038 vs 0.028) is the expected signature of stalled flow being harder to predict than attached flow.

How to read the benchmark

These are comparisons against published experimental datasets, not measurements taken with Riyah. A defensible benchmark also reports the polar source and Reynolds numbers, which corrections were active, and the convergence tolerance — the versioned comparison plots and input files belong with the project report.

08 · Reproducibility & limits

What BEM cannot tell you

Inherent model limitations

  • Annuli are treated as independent — no radial flow.
  • Steady, uniform, axial inflow: no yaw, shear, tower shadow or turbulence without extra models.
  • No dynamic stall or unsteady aerodynamics.
  • Tip and hub corrections are empirical approximations, not resolved vortex physics.
  • Deep-stall behaviour depends on the extrapolation model chosen.
  • Rigid blade — no aeroelastic deflection changing the effective geometry.

Reproducibility practice

  • Version the polar data alongside the results — polars change the answer more than solver settings do.
  • Record which corrections and extrapolation model were active.
  • State units and reference area explicitly on every coefficient.
  • Report convergence tolerance and element count, and check element-count sensitivity.
  • Keep benchmark inputs as fixed, re-runnable cases.

Where I want to take it

  • Yawed and sheared inflow — skewed-wake correction and a proper inflow model.
  • AEP-driven optimisation with site wind distributions and a tip-speed/noise constraint, rather than single-point CP.
  • Published benchmark plots — the MEXICO and Phase VI comparison curves with versioned inputs, alongside the RMSE figures.
  • Cross-fidelity check — compare BEM against rotor CFD at selected operating points to quantify where the fast model drifts.
  • Documentation and tests — a documented module API and a regression suite around the built-in validation cases.
